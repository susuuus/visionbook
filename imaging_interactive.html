Pinhole Camera Geometry & Perspective Projection

The pinhole camera is the simplest model of image formation. Light rays travel in straight lines from the 3D world, pass through a small pinhole aperture, and project onto a 2D projection plane, creating an inverted image.

BRDF: Light Reflection & Surface Properties

The BRDF (Bidirectional Reflectance Distribution Function) describes how light reflects off a surface depending on the viewing and lighting directions. It’s fundamental to understanding image formation and how surfaces appear under different illumination.

Similar Triangles & Perspective Projection Derivation

The fundamental perspective projection equation comes from similar triangles geometry. The relationship between world coordinates and image coordinates follows from the pinhole camera geometry.

Orthographic vs Perspective Projection

Two fundamentally different projection models produce different results. Orthographic projection (parallel rays) preserves object size regardless of distance, while perspective projection (converging rays at the pinhole) makes distant objects appear smaller.

Why Walls Don’t Show Images: The Importance of the Pinhole

Without any restriction on light, walls receive scattered light from all directions and angles, creating no clear image. The pinhole solves this by restricting light to a single ray per point, creating a sharp image.

Pinhole Camera Coordinate Systems

The pinhole camera model uses three coordinate systems: world coordinates (the real 3D world), camera coordinates (relative to the camera), and image coordinates (2D pixels on the sensor).

Sphere Rendering: Lambertian vs Phong Materials

Different reflection models produce different visual appearances. Lambertian materials are purely diffuse (matte), while Phong materials add specular highlights (shiny, mirror-like reflections).
